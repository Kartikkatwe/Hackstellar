Total token saving is smaller than the cost saving, because most of our tokens go to a decision model that is far cheaper per token.

Picks from options, writes nothingfast · cheap

The LLM writes only when something new needs a name.

An LLM is a writer: you pay for text and wait for text. Filing a complaint is not writing; it is choices from options we already have. Jev, a decision model, picks an option and returns probabilities, and writes no output tokens. Sure: filed. Unsure: it says so and we pass it on. Cheapest step first: a free local match, Jev's yes or no, and only the last few reach the LLM, whose real job is naming new problems and merging duplicates.
How Jev works, in general. We give it the text and some questions, and each question carries its own answer space. A noul question is yes or no, and Jev says how likely yes is. A choice question lists the options, and Jev gives a probability for every one. A score question is an ordered scale, and Jev gives a point on it. One call answers all of them. Nothing comes back as free text, so there is nothing to parse and almost nothing to pay for, and every answer says how sure it is.

Left to right. A complaint arrives by email, call, chat or social media. Intake turns the four formats into one and finds the customer. Then one call to Jev answers the questions at once: is it a complaint (if not, it is set aside), which of the business's areas, how severe, was money lost, plus mood and legal risk. Similarity, free on our machine, compares it with the known problems. A close match is filed straight away. Clearly new: it goes to the waiting list. Not sure: Jev re-checks with a yes or no. Yes: filed. No: waiting list. Still not sure: only now does the LLM decide. When three different customers report the same new thing, the LLM names it as a new problem. The cheapest check always goes first.

About 60 seconds; say it like you are thinking out loud. First, the scale: quick commerce in India does around 7.8 million orders a day. Even what reaches the government is big: the consumer helpline was taking about five thousand calls a day by the end of last year, more than ten times 2015. In one year Swiggy alone had about 9,500 complaints escalated to the government, Zomato about 7,000, Flipkart over one and a half lakh. And those are only the people who gave up on the company. The complaints companies handle themselves are far bigger: probably 80,000 to 2 lakh a day in quick commerce alone, by our estimate. The expensive part is already solved: most complaints never touch the LLM, so cost grows in a straight line; at ten million complaints a month it is around four hundred dollars in model calls. The classifying step shares no data, so we just add workers; one worker does roughly fifteen complaints a second, and 80,000 to 2 lakh a day is only one to three a second on average, so a company like Swiggy fits on a server or two. The only tricky bit is the issue list, because two workers could create the same issue twice, so each business area gets its own single writer, all running side by side. New customer: new partitions. One area gets slammed, like payments during an outage: we split it. Honestly, it is not built at that scale yet; the first thing we would do is load-test it.
