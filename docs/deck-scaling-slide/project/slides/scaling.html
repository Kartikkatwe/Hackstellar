Let me start with how big this is. In India, quick-commerce apps get about 7.8 million orders every day. Some of those go wrong, and people complain. We can see part of it in government data. The national consumer helpline gets about five thousand calls a day. In one year, Swiggy had about 9,500 complaints there, and Zomato about 7,000. And it’s the same few problems again and again: refunds that never came, late deliveries, damaged items, payments that failed. Those are exactly the problems our system catches early. And that’s only what reaches the government. Most people just complain to the app directly. We estimate 80,000 to 2 lakh complaints a day in quick commerce. That sounds huge, but it’s only one to three complaints a second. So can we handle it? Yes. Reading a complaint is the slow part, and it doesn’t share anything, so we just add more workers. One worker reads about fifteen complaints a second. So one company fits on one or two servers. Saving the complaint under the right issue is the only tricky part, because two workers could create the same issue twice. So each area, like payments or delivery, gets its own single worker, and all the areas work at the same time. A new company just adds new areas. If one area gets too busy, we split it. And it stays cheap. Most complaints never need the expensive AI, so at ten million complaints a month we pay about four hundred dollars. It’s not built at that size yet, so the first thing we’ll do is a load test.
